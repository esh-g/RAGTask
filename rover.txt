# NASA Mars Surface Rovers: Missions, Engineering, and Instruments

## Overview

Since 1997, NASA has landed five wheeled robotic vehicles on the surface of Mars, along with one small helicopter. This reference describes each mission's landing site, engineering design, scientific instruments, and principal findings, drawing on public mission documentation published by NASA and the Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

The five rovers are Sojourner, delivered by the Mars Pathfinder mission in 1997; the twin Mars Exploration Rovers, Spirit and Opportunity, which landed three weeks apart in January 2004; Curiosity, the centerpiece of the Mars Science Laboratory mission, which arrived in August 2012; and Perseverance, part of the Mars 2020 mission, which touched down in February 2021. Curiosity and Perseverance are both still operating.

Together they trace a clear arc in mission ambition. The first was built simply to prove that a vehicle could be driven at all across terrain nobody had ever visited. The second generation went looking for evidence that liquid water had once been present. The third asked whether the planet had ever been habitable, in the specific sense of possessing the chemistry and conditions that microbial life would require. The fourth and current generation goes further still, hunting directly for preserved biosignatures in ancient sediment and sealing rock cores into tubes so that a future mission might carry them back to laboratories on Earth. Each step required not only better instruments but substantially better landing precision, because the scientifically interesting sites are almost always the geologically rough ones. A flat, boring plain is easy to land on and tells you very little; a river delta wedged against a crater wall is exactly the opposite on both counts. Much of the engineering history of the program is really a history of shrinking the landing ellipse until the best sites came within reach.

Every one of these vehicles shares a common piece of engineering heritage: the rocker-bogie suspension. This six-wheeled passive suspension system allows a rover to keep all six wheels in contact with uneven ground without springs or shock absorbers, and it lets a vehicle climb over an obstacle roughly the size of its own wheel while keeping the body relatively level. The design has been used on every NASA Mars rover without exception, scaled up from Sojourner's shoebox proportions to Perseverance's car-sized frame.

The rovers also share a communications architecture. Rather than transmitting all their data directly to Earth, they relay most of it through spacecraft in Mars orbit, which have larger antennas and more available power than anything on the surface. Mars Odyssey, the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, and MAVEN all serve as relays for NASA's surface assets, as does the European Space Agency's Trace Gas Orbiter. A rover typically gets only a few short relay passes per Martian day. This is one reason the vehicles are designed to execute long command sequences autonomously rather than being driven in real time, and it is also why onboard power matters so much: a vehicle that cannot keep its electronics warm through the night will not survive to use its next communications window regardless of how good its instruments are.

## Sojourner

Sojourner was the first wheeled vehicle to operate on another planet. It arrived aboard the Mars Pathfinder lander, which touched down in Ares Vallis on July 4, 1997, and it was named for the abolitionist and women's rights advocate Sojourner Truth.

By later standards it was tiny. It weighed about 11.5 kilograms and measured roughly 65 centimeters long, 48 centimeters wide, and 30 centimeters tall, which is approximately the size of a microwave oven. It ran on a small solar panel supplemented by non-rechargeable lithium batteries, and its top speed was around one centimeter per second. It never traveled more than about 12 meters from the lander, which served as its communications base station, and it covered roughly 100 meters in total.

Pathfinder used an unusual landing method that became a signature of the era. Rather than firing retrorockets all the way to the ground, the spacecraft inflated a cluster of giant airbags, cut its parachute loose, and bounced across the Martian surface more than a dozen times before rolling to a stop. The lander then opened its three petals, righted itself, and deployed ramps for the rover to drive down. The lander was later renamed the Carl Sagan Memorial Station.

The science payload was modest. Its primary instrument was an Alpha Proton X-ray Spectrometer, which was pressed directly against rocks and soil to determine their elemental composition. The rover also carried three cameras: two black-and-white units on the front for navigation and imaging, and a color camera at the rear. Two engineering experiments rounded out the payload. A wheel abrasion experiment used strips of aluminum, nickel, and platinum mounted on one wheel to study how Martian soil wears down materials, and a materials adhesion experiment measured how much dust accumulated on different surfaces over time.

It carried no drill, no grinding tool, and no sample-handling hardware of any kind. Anything it wanted to analyze, it had to analyze exactly as it found it, dust and weathered rind included.

The rover was designed to last seven sols, or Martian days. It operated for 83, returning 550 images and analyzing the composition of a series of rocks that mission scientists nicknamed Barnacle Bill, Yogi, and Scooby-Doo. The chemistry of those rocks suggested a more varied geological history than expected, including material resembling terrestrial andesite. Communications were lost on September 27, 1997, most likely because the lander's battery failed.

Its real contribution was operational rather than scientific. It demonstrated that a small, semi-autonomous vehicle could be safely delivered to the surface, commanded from Earth across a light-delay of many minutes, and driven across genuinely unknown terrain without being lost.

## Spirit

The Mars Exploration Rovers were built as an identical pair and sent to opposite sides of the planet. Spirit, designated MER-A, landed in Gusev Crater on January 4, 2004. Its twin followed three weeks later. Both were delivered using the same airbag-bouncing technique Pathfinder had pioneered, though at a considerably larger scale.

Each rover weighed about 185 kilograms and stood roughly 1.5 meters tall, with a body about the size of a golf cart. Both were solar powered, drawing electricity from panels that unfolded after landing, with rechargeable lithium-ion batteries to carry them through the night. The prime mission was 90 sols apiece.

Spirit carried a mast-mounted Panoramic Camera, known as Pancam, a pair of high-resolution cameras with a filter wheel allowing multispectral imaging of the landscape. Beside it sat the Miniature Thermal Emission Spectrometer, or Mini-TES, which analyzed infrared emissions from rocks and soil at a distance to identify minerals and pick out targets worth driving to. Navigation and hazard-avoidance cameras handled the practical business of driving. On the robotic arm were four tools mounted on a turret: a Microscopic Imager for extreme close-up photography at a resolution fine enough to resolve individual sand grains, an Alpha Particle X-ray Spectrometer for elemental chemistry, a Mössbauer Spectrometer specialized in identifying iron-bearing minerals, and the Rock Abrasion Tool, universally called the RAT, a grinding wheel that scraped away the weathered outer rind of a rock to expose fresh material underneath.

Gusev Crater turned out to be floored with volcanic basalt rather than the lakebed sediments the team had hoped for, so the rover drove several kilometers to the Columbia Hills to find older rock. There it found some of the mission's most striking results, including a patch of nearly pure silica near a feature called Home Plate. On Earth, deposits like that form in hot springs or fumaroles, pointing to past hydrothermal activity — heat and water together, a combination of considerable interest to anyone thinking about habitability.

In 2009 it broke through a crust of hardened soil and became embedded in soft material at a site the team named Troy. Efforts to extract it failed, and it was reclassified as a stationary research platform. Its last communication was on March 22, 2010, and NASA ended recovery attempts in May 2011. It had driven 7.73 kilometers.

## Opportunity

Opportunity, designated MER-B, landed in Meridiani Planum on January 25, 2004. It was mechanically identical to its twin and carried the same instrument suite: Pancam and Mini-TES on the mast, and a Microscopic Imager, an Alpha Particle X-ray Spectrometer, a Mössbauer Spectrometer, and the Rock Abrasion Tool on the arm turret.

It delivered a decisive result almost immediately. The lander happened to bounce into a small depression later named Eagle Crater, where exposed bedrock walls contained layered sedimentary rock studded with small gray spheres that the team nicknamed blueberries. The Mössbauer Spectrometer identified them as hematite concretions, and the surrounding rock contained the mineral jarosite. Both minerals form in the presence of water, and jarosite specifically requires acidic water. Within weeks of landing, the mission had established that the site had once been soaked.

It then spent fourteen years driving. It examined progressively larger craters, working outward from Eagle to Endurance, then Victoria, and finally the 22-kilometer Endeavour Crater, where it found clay minerals that form in less acidic water than the jarosite deposits, suggesting an earlier and more hospitable era. Along the way it survived dust storms, a stuck front wheel that forced it to drive backward for years, an arm joint failure, and the loss of its flash memory, each of which was worked around by operators rewriting flight procedures around the damage rather than repairing anything.

It ultimately drove 45.16 kilometers, which is still the record for any vehicle on another world. Contact was lost on June 10, 2018, when a planet-encircling dust storm blocked enough sunlight to starve its solar panels. NASA formally concluded the mission on February 13, 2019.

## Curiosity

Curiosity was a step change in scale. At 899 kilograms it weighed nearly five times as much as one of the Mars Exploration Rovers, and at about three meters long it is roughly the size of a small car. It landed in Gale Crater on August 6, 2012, and it is still operating.

Its size made the airbag method impossible, so the Mars Science Laboratory mission introduced a new arrival technique. After the heat shield and parachute had done their work, a rocket-powered descent stage separated, slowed to a hover about twenty meters above the surface, and lowered the vehicle to the ground on nylon tethers. Once the wheels touched down and the tethers went slack, the cables were cut and the descent stage flew off to crash a safe distance away. Engineers called the maneuver the sky crane. The entire sequence was fully autonomous, because Mars was far enough away that the landing was over before the first radio signal announcing atmospheric entry had reached Earth.

It is also the first Mars rover not to depend on sunlight. Power comes from a Multi-Mission Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator, which converts heat from the decay of plutonium-238 into roughly 110 watts of electricity. This frees the vehicle from seasonal power constraints and from dust accumulation on solar panels, and the waste heat helps keep instruments warm through the Martian night. It is the main reason the mission has now run for well over a decade.

The mission was framed around habitability rather than life directly: could Gale Crater once have supported microbial life? Ten instruments were selected accordingly.

The Mast Camera, or Mastcam, is a pair of color cameras providing the primary imaging of the landscape, and it can shoot high-definition video. Mounted beside it is the Chemistry and Camera instrument, ChemCam, which is one of the more theatrical pieces of hardware on Mars: it fires an infrared laser at a rock up to seven meters away, vaporizing a pinhead-sized spot into glowing plasma, then reads the light from that plasma with a spectrograph to determine the rock's elemental composition. The technique is called laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy, and it lets the science team assess a target without spending days driving to it.

On the robotic arm are the Mars Hand Lens Imager, MAHLI, a focusable close-up camera modeled on a geologist's hand lens, and an Alpha Particle X-ray Spectrometer for contact chemistry. The arm also carries a percussive drill that bores into rock and delivers powdered samples into the rover's body.

That powder goes to two laboratory instruments inside the chassis, and these are where most of the mission's headline results have come from. Chemistry and Mineralogy, CheMin, uses X-ray diffraction to identify the specific minerals present, which is a considerably more definitive measurement than elemental chemistry alone: knowing that a rock contains iron, sulfur, and oxygen tells you much less than knowing it contains jarosite. Sample Analysis at Mars, SAM, is the largest and most complex instrument aboard, occupying roughly half the payload mass. SAM combines a quadrupole mass spectrometer, a gas chromatograph, and a tunable laser spectrometer in a single package, and it is designed to detect organic compounds and to measure the isotopic composition of atmospheric and evolved gases. It works by heating a sample to around 900 degrees Celsius in one of dozens of small cups and analyzing the gases that come off. Using this approach, SAM detected chlorobenzene and other complex organic molecules preserved in three-billion-year-old mudstone at a site called Cumberland, the first unambiguous detection of organics in Martian rock. It also measured seasonal fluctuations in atmospheric methane that have never been satisfactorily explained, and its isotopic measurements of argon confirmed that certain meteorites recovered on Earth genuinely originated on Mars. Because the number of sample cups is finite and several were expended early, the team now treats each drilled sample delivery to SAM as a significant strategic decision rather than a routine measurement.

Three further instruments monitor the environment. The Radiation Assessment Detector measures the radiation a human crew would be exposed to, both during the cruise to Mars and on the surface. Dynamic Albedo of Neutrons uses a neutron source to detect hydrogen, and therefore water or hydrated minerals, in the ground beneath the vehicle. The Rover Environmental Monitoring Station is a weather station tracking temperature, pressure, humidity, wind, and ultraviolet radiation. A tenth instrument, the Mars Descent Imager, filmed the landing itself.

The central finding came early. Drilling into mudstone at a site called Yellowknife Bay, the mission found clay minerals along with sulfur, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and carbon, in rock that had clearly been deposited in calm, neutral-pH water. Gale Crater had once held a long-lived lake system.

Most of the mission since has been spent climbing Mount Sharp, the five-kilometer layered mound at the center of Gale Crater, reading the mineralogical record upward through time. More recently it has been working through a region of boxwork terrain, where ridges of erosion-resistant material form a honeycomb pattern thought to have been deposited by groundwater flowing through fractures.

The most visible engineering problem has been wheel damage. The thin aluminum wheels accumulated dents and punctures far faster than expected after encountering sharp embedded rocks, and drivers now deliberately route around such terrain and use a traction control algorithm that adjusts individual wheel speeds to reduce stress on the treads.

## Perseverance

Perseverance landed in Jezero Crater on February 18, 2021. It shares Curiosity's basic chassis and its radioisotope power source, and at 1,025 kilograms it is slightly heavier, largely because of a much heavier instrument turret and a coring drill on the end of its robotic arm.

Jezero was chosen because orbital imagery shows an unmistakable river delta spilling into the crater, a fan of sediment deposited where an ancient river emptied into a lake. Deltas are excellent places to look for preserved biosignatures, because fine-grained sediment settles out of slow-moving water and can entomb organic material before it decays.

Landing there required more precision than previous missions could offer, so Mars 2020 added two capabilities to the descent system inherited from Curiosity. Range Trigger deploys the parachute based on the spacecraft's position rather than simply its velocity, tightening the landing ellipse considerably. Terrain-Relative Navigation gives the descent stage a camera and an onboard map, so it can recognize the ground beneath it during descent and steer toward a safe patch. Together they made a hazardous site like Jezero viable.

The rover carries seven science instruments and a total of 23 cameras, more than any previous interplanetary mission, along with two microphones that captured the first sounds ever recorded on another planet.

Mastcam-Z is the primary imaging system, a stereo pair with a 28-to-100 millimeter optical zoom. SuperCam, mounted on the mast alongside it, is an expanded successor to ChemCam that combines laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy with Raman spectroscopy, visible and infrared spectroscopy, a color camera, and one of the two microphones.

Two instruments sit on the arm turret for close-up work. The Planetary Instrument for X-ray Lithochemistry, PIXL, is a micro-focus X-ray fluorescence spectrometer that maps elemental chemistry at a scale fine enough to resolve features the size of a grain of salt, paired with its own close-up camera. SHERLOC uses an ultraviolet laser together with Raman and fluorescence spectroscopy to detect organic compounds and minerals; its context imager is named WATSON. The acronym stands for Scanning Habitable Environments with Raman and Luminescence for Organics and Chemicals, and the instrument suite was deliberately designed with overlapping capabilities so that the loss of any single spectrometer would not prevent the mission from meeting its science objectives.

The remaining three instruments look outward rather than at rocks. The Mars Environmental Dynamics Analyzer, MEDA, is a weather station measuring temperature, pressure, humidity, wind, radiation, and airborne dust. The Radar Imager for Mars' Subsurface Experiment, RIMFAX, is a ground-penetrating radar that images rock layers up to about ten meters below the surface. The Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment, MOXIE, was a technology demonstration rather than a science instrument: a toaster-sized unit that heated Martian atmospheric carbon dioxide to around 800 °C and used solid oxide electrolysis to split off oxygen. Between April 2021 and its final run on August 7, 2023, MOXIE produced 122 grams of oxygen in total, peaking at 12 grams per hour at a purity of 98 percent or better, roughly twice its design goal.

The defining task is sample caching, and it shapes almost everything about how the mission operates. The rover carries 43 sample tubes, each machined to exacting cleanliness standards because the entire scientific value of a returned sample depends on being certain that any organic molecule found inside it came from Mars rather than from a factory floor in California. The tubes were baked, cleaned, and sealed on Earth, and a subset of them were designated witness tubes, opened on Mars but never filled with rock, so that scientists can later characterize whatever terrestrial contamination survived the journey and subtract it from their results. The coring drill on the arm turret extracts cylindrical rock cores roughly the size of a piece of chalk, which are then transferred inside the rover's body, imaged, hermetically sealed, and stored in a carousel. The site selection process for each core is deliberate and slow: the science team typically uses the mast instruments to survey a region, then the arm instruments to examine a specific patch of rock in detail, and only then commits to a core, because tubes are finite and cannot be emptied and reused. In December 2022 and January 2023 the rover deposited a backup depot of ten sealed tubes on the ground at a site called Three Forks, laid out in a carefully surveyed pattern so that a future retrieval mission could locate and collect them even if the rover itself had failed by then. This was purely an insurance measure. The remaining tubes are still aboard, and the baseline plan has always been for the rover to hand them over directly. What that retrieval mission will look like has changed repeatedly since launch, as the architecture and budget for returning the samples to Earth have been reassessed, and no firm date for their arrival has been established.

The mission has evolved considerably since landing. After surveying the crater floor and the delta, the rover climbed out of Jezero entirely, ascending the crater rim on sol 1358 in December 2024 and reaching sites named Soroya Ridge and Witch Hazel Hill.

Its wheel steering actuators have been certified for at least another 60 kilometers of driving, well beyond the 20 kilometers the vehicle was originally qualified for. This is a direct consequence of the wheel problems encountered by its predecessor: the newer wheels are thicker, narrower, and larger in diameter, with curved rather than chevron-shaped treads.

## Ingenuity

Ingenuity flew to Mars strapped to Perseverance's belly and was set down on the surface in April 2021. It was a technology demonstration with a single objective: find out whether powered flight is possible in an atmosphere with roughly one percent the density of Earth's.

The problem is severe. Thin air means rotors must spin extremely fast and blades must be very large relative to the vehicle's mass. Ingenuity weighed just 1.8 kilograms, used two counter-rotating rotors about 1.2 meters across spinning at more than 2,500 revolutions per minute, roughly five times the rate of a terrestrial helicopter, and recharged from a small solar panel. Unlike the rovers of its generation it was not nuclear powered, and much of its energy budget went to simply keeping its electronics warm overnight rather than to flying.

It carried no science instruments. Its two cameras existed for navigation and for documenting terrain, not for research.

Its first flight, on April 19, 2021, lasted about 39 seconds and rose three meters. It was the first powered, controlled flight by an aircraft on another planet, and a small piece of fabric from the Wright brothers' 1903 Flyer was attached to the vehicle in acknowledgment.

It was planned to make up to five flights over 30 days. It made 72 over nearly three years, gradually shifting from a demonstration to an operational scout that flew ahead of the rover to photograph terrain and identify routes and science targets. It flew more than 17 kilometers in total and reached altitudes of 24 meters.

The mission ended on January 18, 2024. During a flight over featureless, low-contrast sandy terrain, its navigation system struggled to track surface features, and it landed hard enough to damage the rotor blade tips. NASA announced its retirement on January 25, 2024. It remains where it landed, and Perseverance has photographed it from a distance.

## Comparative Summary

Sojourner, 1997. Mass 11.5 kilograms. Solar powered. Landed by airbag in Ares Vallis. One spectrometer and three cameras. Operated 83 sols against a 7-sol design life. Drove approximately 100 meters.

Spirit, 2004. Mass 185 kilograms. Solar powered. Landed by airbag in Gusev Crater. Five science instruments plus the Rock Abrasion Tool. Operated more than 2,200 sols against a 90-sol design life. Drove 7.73 kilometers before becoming embedded in soft soil.

Opportunity, 2004. Identical to Spirit. Landed in Meridiani Planum. Operated more than 5,100 sols. Drove 45.16 kilometers, the greatest distance traveled by any vehicle on another world.

Curiosity, 2012. Mass 899 kilograms. Radioisotope powered. Landed by sky crane in Gale Crater. Ten science instruments including two internal laboratories. Still active after more than 4,900 sols.

Perseverance, 2021. Mass 1,025 kilograms. Radioisotope powered. Landed by sky crane with terrain-relative navigation in Jezero Crater. Seven science instruments and 23 cameras. Caches rock cores for potential future return to Earth. Still active.

Ingenuity, 2021. Mass 1.8 kilograms. Solar powered. Deployed from Perseverance. Two cameras and no science instruments. Completed 72 flights against a 5-flight plan. Retired after blade damage in January 2024.
