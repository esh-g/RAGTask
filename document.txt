A Mars rover is a remote-controlled motor vehicle designed to travel on the surface of Mars. Rovers offer several advantages over stationary landers: they examine more territory, can be directed to interesting geological features, can position themselves in sunny spots to weather winter months, and advance the engineering knowledge of remote robotic vehicle control. They serve a distinct purpose compared to orbital spacecraft like the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, while newer exploration efforts include aerial platforms such as the Mars helicopter.

There have been six successful robotically operated Mars rovers. The first five, managed by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, include Sojourner (1997), Spirit (2004–2010), Opportunity (2004–2018), Curiosity (2012–present), and Perseverance (2021–present). The sixth, managed by the China National Space Administration, is Zhurong (2021–2022). A primary objective across these missions has been the search for evidence of ancient life and planetary habitability, including ancient fluvio-lacustrine environments like river plains and lake beds, organic carbon, and taphonomic evidence related to fossil preservation.

Early Soviet probes like Mars 2 and Mars 3 relied on physical tethers, while NASA's Sojourner depended on the Mars Pathfinder base station to relay communications to Earth. Later rovers, including Spirit, Opportunity, Curiosity, and Perseverance, were built with independent direct-to-Earth and orbital relay communication systems. While earlier missions completed their operations after years of service or lost contact due to severe environmental conditions, Curiosity and Perseverance continue active scientific operations on the Martian surface.

Curiosity, part of NASA's Mars Science Laboratory mission, launched on November 26, 2011, and landed on the Aeolis Palus plain near Mount Sharp in Gale Crater on August 6, 2012. It carries a suite of analytical instruments designed to assess whether Mars ever had an environment capable of supporting microbial life. Perseverance, launched with the Mars 2020 mission on July 30, 2020, landed in Jezero Crater on February 18, 2021. Based on Curiosity's core mechanical design, Perseverance carried the Ingenuity helicopter on its belly, seeks direct biosignatures, and caches rock and soil cores for future return missions.

Among past missions, the small Sojourner rover landed on July 4, 1997, and operated until communications ceased in September 1997, traveling roughly 100 meters. The twin Mars Exploration Rovers, Spirit and Opportunity, landed in January 2004. Spirit traveled 7.73 kilometers over six years before becoming trapped in loose sand in 2010. Opportunity set major planetary longevity records, operating for 5,352 sols (nearly 15 Earth years) and driving 45.16 kilometers. Opportunity's mission concluded in 2018 when an intense global dust storm blocked the solar radiation needed to recharge its batteries.

China's Zhurong rover landed in southern Utopia Planitia on May 14, 2021, as part of the Tianwen-1 mission, making China the second nation to operate a rover successfully on Mars. Designed for a 90-sol lifespan, Zhurong operated for 347 sols and covered 1,921 meters before entering hibernation in May 2022. Heavy Martian dust accumulation on its solar arrays prevented reactivation. Earlier historical attempts include the Soviet 1971 Mars 2 and Mars 3 missions carrying small ski-mounted PrOP-M rovers; Mars 2 crashed upon descent, and Mars 3 failed roughly two minutes after touching down during a massive regional dust storm.

Future exploration concepts and planned missions continue to evolve. The European Space Agency's ExoMars rover, Rosalind Franklin, is designed to drill deep beneath the Martian surface to sample un-irradiated soil. The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) has proposed a rover for its Mars Lander Mission. Additional conceptual architectures include wind-propelled spherical tumbleweed rovers designed to cover vast geographic areas driven by Martian atmospheric winds.

NASA structures its Mars rover program around technological mission objectives and scientific objectives. The primary science goals of the long-term Mars Exploration Program comprise four key pillars: determining whether life ever arose on Mars, characterizing the Martian climate, characterizing the geology of the planet, and preparing for future human exploration missions. Instruments onboard the rovers—ranging from spectrometers and sub-surface radars to atmospheric sensors and oxygen production experiments—are tailored directly to meet these scientific goals.
